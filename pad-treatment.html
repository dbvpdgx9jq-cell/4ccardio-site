Signs of a blocked leg artery

Pain when you walk

Cramping or tiredness in the calf, thigh or buttock that comes on with walking and goes away when you stop. This is called claudication, and many people mistake it for arthritis or getting older.

A wound that will not heal

A sore on the foot, toe or shin that has been open for weeks, especially with diabetes. Without enough blood flow, even good wound care cannot close it.

Pain at night or a cold foot

Foot pain that wakes you up and eases when you hang the leg off the bed, or a foot that is cold, pale or has no pulse. This is the most serious stage and needs prompt care.

How it works

From ultrasound to open artery

Most patients need two visits: one to find the problem, one to fix it. The procedure is done through a small puncture with local anesthesia and sedation, not surgery.

Call to schedule
  1. Office visit with an arterial ultrasound and ankle-brachial index (ABI) to measure blood flow in each leg.
  2. If there is a blockage, an angiogram in our Doral lab images the arteries from the aorta to the foot, and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) measures each vessel from the inside.
  3. The blockage is treated in the same session with a balloon, a stent, atherectomy to remove plaque, lithotripsy to break up calcium, or thrombectomy to remove clot.
  4. You rest for a few hours and go home the same day, with a follow-up visit and ultrasound to check the result.

Questions patients ask

What is peripheral artery disease?

PAD is a narrowing or blockage of the arteries that carry blood to the legs, caused by the same plaque that causes heart attacks. It reduces blood flow to the muscles and skin of the leg and foot.

How do I know if I have it?

A painless test in our office, the ankle-brachial index (ABI), compares blood pressure in your ankles and arms. An arterial ultrasound then shows exactly where any blockage is. Both take less than an hour.

Is the procedure surgery?

No. It is done through a small puncture, usually in the groin, with local anesthesia and light sedation. There is no incision to heal, and you go home the same day.

How long is recovery?

Most people walk the same evening and return to normal activity within a few days. Your doctor will tell you about any limits for the puncture site.

I have diabetes and a foot wound. Should I be checked?

Yes. People with diabetes are at the highest risk for PAD, and poor blood flow is a common reason wounds do not heal. Ask your podiatrist to send you, or call us directly.

My doctor mentioned amputation. Is it too late?

Not necessarily. Many amputations happen without an angiogram ever being done. Restoring blood flow can heal wounds and save the leg. Call us for an evaluation before any decision is made.

Is PAD treatment covered by insurance?

Yes. Evaluation and treatment of PAD are covered by Medicare and most plans when medically necessary. We verify your coverage and copay before your appointment.
